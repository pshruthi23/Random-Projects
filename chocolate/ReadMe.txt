This is another mini project of mine. I found another food-related dataset to work on- the chocolate dataset from Kaggle. It was definitely very difficult to work on this without getting an intense craving, but I managed to make it till the end with the help of ice cream!
To analyze this data set, I used Google BigQuery and visualized the results in Google Data Studio (previously known as Looker Studio). 

Data Preprocessing:
I downloaded 5 CSV files from Kaggle and imported them into the BigQuery environment. After importing it, I realized the column headers did not get imported, and I had to change the names from generic ones - string_field_1, string_field_2, etc. I had to write some data transformation SQL code to restore the header names to the original that was there in the CSV files. 

Once I got that all done, my next challenge came. The city column in the stores table was not mapped to the right country column. I tried writing another Data Transformation code, but that functionality was not possible in the free version of BigQuery. 

I then used Excel (another miracle software!) to get the unique cities (UNIQUE) and wrote a cute little VLOOKUP to get the right mapping. I then re-imported the right table into BigQuery. 

The pre-processing took longer than expected, with a few extra steps. 

Analysis: 
I had a few questions in mind that I wanted to explore. 

I started with the easiest and most obvious - Sales Trends. For each store over the years, I wanted to see the total number of customers, the inventory sold, and the profits. I extracted the year from the order date in the sales table and used that for the year. Store 72 in 2024 had the highest profit; however, the store that sold the highest inventory was Store 33 in 2024.

I then moved on to the most popular chocolate. My definition of popular is the product that was sold the most. It was the Mars White Chocolate (I am obsessed with White Chocolate <3) with 74066 items being sold. 

I heard that retail stores are the most convenient to shop at (and sensible people shop there), online is the cheapest and offers the most discounts, and adventurous people shop at airports. To test this, I looked to see which stores sold the most, had the highest profits and revenue, and sold the most items. Based on my analysis, the airport stores had the highest sales, with 896417 products sold. Airports again had the highest total profits at $3046173.73; however, the profits per retail store were the highest! 
The revenue generated per mall store was again the highest at $254931.86. 

I wanted to see who purchases the most chocolate, men or women. According to the analysis, women and men both purchased about the same amount from Airports, at ~17 units per customer. Men and women also spent the same amount (creating the same revenue), with the highest being in Airport stores. I guess cravings hit hard at airports when you are going to miss someone or anticipating something exciting on a trip!

Another dimension of the data I wanted to explore was the effects of loyalty points. Is it really the instigator of shopping more? I think my answer based on this analysis would be 'No'. There are almost as many women who are and are not part of the loyalty program. There are more men in this dataset's loyalty program. Women who weren't part of the loyalty program bought the most chocolates at ~60 units per customer, and generated the highest revenue. 

Which season is actually the chocolate season? I would assume Winter (December - February) with the holiday season and Valentine's Day. According to the dataset, the average revenue and discounts during the season were pretty much the same. 

I was curious to see store performance by City/ Country. European Stores - London and Paris had the highest number of stores at 16 in each city. Overall, the most chocolate was sold in Toronto, and the highest units sold per store were in New York, which also had the highest revenue and profits per store!

Which brands have the highest profits? The answer was Ferrero; however, under its brand name, there are only 18 products compared to Cadbury, Godiva, and Hershey, with 20 products under their label. 

Lastly, I wanted to see the purchasing behavior of different age groups. The highest revenue per person was generated by the 65+ age group, which bought 60 chocolates each.

This was a very long analysis, as I wanted to cover the maximum ways to look at the data (as much as I could). As usual, Claude AI was very helpful with its suggestions :P
